## 10a. The invisible-secondary test

**The argument.** In a circular orbit both conjunctions cover the same overlap area, so the secondary-to-primary depth ratio is the two stars' TESS-band surface-brightness ratio (run 9d). Under the hypothesis that the adopted P is the orbit and the second eclipse is too faint to see:

1. The primary depth δ needs a companion of at least R₂ = R₁ √(δ / f), with f = 1.3 allowing for central limb darkening. Grazing geometry, third light and a larger companion all make R₂ bigger, so this is a lower bound.
2. A main-sequence star of that radius has a temperature (Pecaut & Mamajek dwarf sequence), capped at the primary's own TIC temperature.
3. Its occultation must then be at least (δ / f) × S(T₂)/S(T₁), with S the blackbody surface brightness over 600–1,000 nm.
4. The light curve allows at most 3 × 2 × rms / √N in a window of half the fitted duration (×2 for red noise, floor 0.3 ppt), with Earthshine's per-point rms and point counts.

If the minimum secondary exceeds 1.5 times that limit, P is excluded. Companions smaller than 0.13 R☉ (0.22 R☉ in the strict run, to cover inflated hot Jupiters) are allowed to be dark and the row is not tested. TIC radius and temperature are used as they stand: under the hypothesis being tested the companion is faint, so they describe the primary.

**Calibration.** The same bound applied to the 500 rows where a measured secondary already fixes P must never exceed the observed secondary. Among rows whose light curve fits the host (Earthshine's "fits" or "short"), it overshoots by more than 1.5× in 5 of 384 (1.3%), and in 0 of 93 below 3 days; the observed/predicted ratio has its 2.5th percentile at 0.92 there. The 26 overshoots in all categories are almost all rows Earthshine marks eccentric or "no host", where the circular-orbit or host assumption fails, which is why the test is applied only to the equal-dips rows and their host data.

**Result.** Of 121 rows (R946 left out: its text and column already disagree, run 5), 118 have host data and a bound:

| | default (f 1.3, red ×2, dark below 0.13 R☉) | strict (f 1.5, red ×3, dark below 0.22 R☉) |
|---|---:|---:|
| P excluded | 68 (45 confirmed, 23 probable) | 48 (32 confirmed, 16 probable) |
| of which P < 3 d | 34 | 23 |

- **Agreement with the parity test.** Earthshine's per-sector test calls 13 of these rows 2P from unequal alternate depths; this test excludes P for 12 (not R1118, 16 ppt deep, where a small companion can hide). R1117, R1120 and R1084 drop out of the strict column only because their M-dwarf hosts make the minimum companion smaller than 0.22 R☉; parity already settles them.
- **What parity cannot see.** 33 excluded rows are "not shown" by the parity test, and 22 were not testable there. Twins have equal eclipses by definition, so a parity test cannot find them; this one does. Median |odd − even| among the excluded rows with parity data is 2%.
- **Where P survives.** The 50 rows not excluded are mostly shallow (median depth 29 ppt), where a K or M companion can hide; four have a companion small enough to be substellar. P remains possible for them, and so does 2P.
- **Eccentricity.** At P a secondary could be missed if the orbit is eccentric and inclined so that only one conjunction eclipses. Below 3 days orbits are circular (run 6d: none of 94 has |e cos ω| > 0.031), so the 34 short-period exclusions do not depend on this. Above 3 days it is possible but needs a fine-tuned geometry for each star; the parity agreement above argues against it being common.

**Three side results** ([`two_rows.py`](two_rows.py) → `two-rows-output.txt`):
- **R1090**, the one of run 5's six predictions TESS could not test by parity (all four observed eclipses are even cycles). - **R1737**: at Earthshine's adopted 40.46 d a 252-ppt eclipse would need a secondary of at least 96 ppt; this agrees with Earthshine's finding that 80.93 d is favoured.

Three have evolved hosts where the bound does not apply (R1076, R1138, R1651); for R978, R1512, R1504, R1416 and R1225 either the tabled "secondary" is not the occultation, or the host parameters are wrong. **For the paper and the table.**
1. For the 48 rows excluded under the strict settings, adopt 2P (twin eclipses) as the period and give P as the alternative; `period_resolution` can say "no main-sequence companion can hide a secondary eclipse at P (run 10a)". 2. The 57 rows whose text says "SB2 … the twin solution 2P is preferred" but whose `adopted_period_days` is P should adopt what their own text prefers; 30 of them are also excluded here.
3. Section 2 says "the doubled period was tested from alternate-eclipse depths and timing and a search for a secondary eclipse". **What "no secondary" rests on.** The limit in step 4 is what the data allow, not a search result. That no secondary is seen rests on the review's own search (the row's text) and Earthshine's independent one, which found none in any of these rows. Earthshine's secondary search sometimes locks on the primary itself, so a shallow secondary near the limit could have been missed by both; rows with a ratio above five (39) would show a secondary at least five times the noise limit, which a diagnostic sheet cannot miss.

**Limits.** Blackbody surface brightness (molecular bands in M dwarfs can move the ratio by tens of per cent; the strict column allows for that), a main-sequence companion (an evolved companion would be larger and brighter, so this is conservative), TIC radius and temperature (a twin's TIC values are blended, but they describe the hypothesis being tested), and the white-noise secondary limit inflated ×2 or ×3. Earthshine's fits are QLP and SPOC light curves from the house cache; nothing here was remeasured from pixels.

## 10b. R1003: Gaia's orbit has a second solution, a bright 0.86 M☉ tertiary, and it nearly switches the eclipses on by itself

Scripts: [`r1003_heavy_root.py`](r1003_heavy_root.py) (NumPy), [`r1003_nbody_numpy.py`](r1003_nbody_numpy.py) (NumPy; about 5 minutes per tertiary mass), [`fourth_body_window.py`](fourth_body_window.py) (standard library). Input: [`nss.csv`](nss.csv), Fable's ESA Gaia TAP copy already in `run9-tertiaries/`. Outputs: `r1003-heavy-root-output.txt`, `r1003-nbody-numpy-output.txt` (and a 3,000-step convergence run, `r1003-nbody-numpy-0.23-3000steps.txt`), `fourth-body-window-output.txt`.

```
python3 r1003_heavy_root.py nss.csv
python3 r1003_nbody_numpy.py 0.23; python3 r1003_nbody_numpy.py 0.864
python3 fourth_body_window.py
```

Run 9b solved Gaia's photocentre orbit, a₀ = a·(m₃/(M₁₂ + m₃) − β), on the rising branch only. A main-sequence tertiary's photocentre motion rises with mass, peaks (at m₃ ≈ 0.57 M☉ here) and falls again as the tertiary's own light pulls the photocentre back, so the measured a₀ = 0.091 AU is reached twice.

- **Two tertiaries fit Gaia equally well.** For the house's 1.1 + 0.9 M☉ pair: m₃ = 0.23 M☉ (run 9b, dark M dwarf) or **m₃ = 0.86 M☉ (68%: 0.85–0.87), a K dwarf contributing about 21% of the G-band light**. With a 1.0 + 0.8 or 1.2 + 1.0 M☉ pair the heavy root is 0.78 or 0.95 M☉, always about 20% of the light. Run 9b's "robust" 0.23 M☉ was robust only on its branch; Gaia cannot tell the two apart.
- **The heavy tertiary moves the eclipses about three times faster.** The same 48 configurations as run 9c's robustness test, integrated in NumPy (RK4 at P_in/1,500, converged: 3,000 steps changes no value by more than 0.001; no general relativity, whose apsidal period here is ~5,000 yr): m₃ = 0.23 M☉ gives at most 0.037 (median 0.026) (R₁ + R₂) per year; m₃ = 0.864 M☉ gives at most 0.101 (median 0.080). The ratio, about 3, is the secular expectation (precession rate ∝ m₃/(M₁₂ + m₃)).
- **The two integrators agree once the eclipses are measured the same way (section 10d).** My first comparison found this NumPy version lower than run 9c's REBOUND runs (maximum 0.037 against 0.074 for m₃ = 0.23). Section 10d traces that to run 9c's way of reading the impact parameter, which adds a spurious drift; measured exactly, both integrators give the numbers here. Scanning the node offset in 10° steps (228 configurations; 10d) the heavy tertiary reaches at most **0.106 per year** (median 0.10 at mutual inclinations 40–60° and 120–140°), the light one 0.038. Against the 0.11–0.22 per year the switch-on needs (run 7), the heavy root comes within a few per cent of the low end and falls short of the middle by about half; the light root falls short by 3–6 times. So on rates alone the heavy root makes the Gaia tertiary **marginal**: enough only if the switch-on needed the smallest change run 7 allowed (b falling by 0.3 in 2.7 years). Section 10e then measures that change from the eclipse depths (0.33–0.38, so at least 0.125–0.145 per year) and finds that the 2026 depths are not changing at all, which no steady precession fits.
- **A closer fourth body is nearly ruled out.** Mardling–Aarseth stability, applied both to a fourth body around the 2.88-d pair and to that pair-plus-fourth-body inside Gaia's e = 0.49 orbit, leaves no room at all for prograde, near-coplanar orbits; only highly inclined or retrograde orbits at P₄ ≈ 8–17 d survive (and the inner pair's own e ≈ 0.49 narrows that further). Any such body heavier than a few hundredths of a solar mass would make eclipse-timing variations of several minutes on a ~10-day cycle (leading-order dynamical term, (m₄/M)·P_in²/(2πP₄) ≈ 4 min for 0.05 M☉ at 12 d), well above the 100–130 s wobble Fable measured in 2026. So run 9c's option (i) needs a polar or retrograde brown dwarf; the heavy Gaia root nearly does the job with nothing added.
- **How to tell the two roots apart.** The pair's systemic velocity swings with semi-amplitude ≈ 17 km/s on the heavy root and ≈ 5 km/s on the light one (206.6-d period, e = 0.49, sin i = 0.94), so two or three spectra months apart decide it. A 21%-light K dwarf would also show its own lines in a single good spectrum, and it dilutes the eclipses, so the pair's true eclipse depths are about a quarter deeper than observed. Gaia DR4's epoch astrometry and RVs (December) may settle it directly.
- **For the paper.** Replace run 9's R1003 sentence with: "Gaia's photocentre orbit (P = 206.6 d, e = 0.49) admits two main-sequence tertiaries, 0.23 M☉ (faint) or 0.86 M☉ (about a fifth of the light); radial velocities distinguish them (systemic semi-amplitude ≈ 17 vs 5 km/s). Neither explains the eclipses' appearance: that needed the impact parameter to fall by at least 0.125 (R₁ + R₂) per year between 2023 and 2026, faster than either tertiary can precess the pair (at most 0.11 and 0.04 per year), and the 2026 eclipse depths are constant to +0.02 ± 0.03 per year, which a precession lasting centuries cannot produce a year after so fast a change. A perturber acting over years, or a non-geometric cause, is needed." (See 10e.)

**Limits.** G-band light as L ∝ m⁴ for main-sequence stars (as in run 9b); no general relativity (negligible over 6 years, section 10d); point masses without tides; the switch-on rate needed is run 7's estimate. The heavy root also changes run 9b's statement that R1003's tertiary "contributes almost no light".

## 10c. Gaia's companion is big enough, or bright enough, to make every eclipse (a consistency check)

Script: [`companion_size.py`](companion_size.py) (NumPy, pandas), from the repository root: `python3 $D/companion_size.py . reviews/2026-11-24-duet-research-runs-1-4/run8-gaia-conjunctions/conjunction-kind.csv` (it reads every `gaia-geometry.json` and the review registers' depths). Outputs: `companion-size-output.txt`, `companion-size.csv`.

Run 8 used the conjunction an eclipse falls on to estimate, for the population, how many probable Gaia-fold systems are mis-associated (about 15%). This asks the per-system question with Gaia's mass function: can the companion Gaia measured physically make the eclipse?

- **Kind 0 (Gaia's visible star eclipsed, 416 accepted systems with a mass function, TIC radius and depth).** The companion's mass follows from the mass function at i ≈ 90°; a main-sequence companion of that mass blocks at most 1.3 (R₂/R₁)². With generous assumptions (m₁ ≥ 1.6 M☉, radii +20%, a 1.5× margin) 415 of 416 pass; the one exception, R243, has a 4.8 R☉ visible star whose mass is surely above 1.6 M☉. With tight assumptions (the house's m₁, no allowances) 51 fail, but at the same rate in both tiers (12% of confirmed, 14% of probable), so those failures measure the assumptions, mostly too low an m₁ for the visible star, not mis-association.
- **Kind 1 (the companion eclipsed, 47 systems).** The eclipse can take away at most the companion's share of the light; with L ∝ m³ every one passes (largest depth/share 0.90, R304).
- **What it means.** At this precision Gaia's companion is always able to make the eclipse, which supports the association for the confirmed tier and is no evidence against the probable tier; it cannot pick out run 8's estimated one-in-seven blends. A test that could would need the visible star's mass and radius from spectroscopy rather than TIC.
- **For the paper (optional).** "For every accepted Gaia-fold system with a usable orbit, the companion implied by Gaia's mass function is large enough (main-sequence radius) or bright enough to produce the observed eclipse depth."

## 10d. Why run 9c's R1003 rates came out high, and the full mutual-inclination scan

Scripts: [`r1003_sampling.py`](r1003_sampling.py) and [`r1003_node_scan.py`](r1003_node_scan.py) (NumPy; they import `r1003_nbody_numpy.py`; about 5 and 15 minutes per tertiary mass). Outputs: `r1003-sampling-0.23.txt`, `r1003-sampling-0.864.txt`, `r1003-node-scan-0.23.txt`, `r1003-node-scan-0.864.txt`.

```
python3 r1003_sampling.py 0.23 > r1003-sampling-0.23.txt;   python3 r1003_sampling.py 0.864 > r1003-sampling-0.864.txt
python3 r1003_node_scan.py 0.23 > r1003-node-scan-0.23.txt; python3 r1003_node_scan.py 0.864 > r1003-node-scan-0.864.txt
```

- **The cause.** Run 9c read the impact parameter at each conjunction as a straight-line interpolation of the projected separation between two samples P_in/100 apart (P_in/120 in its first script). Near conjunction the separation is V-shaped, √(b² + v²t²), so the interpolation overestimates b by up to v·Δt/2, about 0.3 (R₁ + R₂) for this fast, eccentric orbit, by an amount that depends on where the samples fall relative to the conjunction. That phase drifts slowly from eclipse to eclipse, so the error drifts too and adds a spurious slope.
- **The test.** One integration, three ways of reading b: run 9c's interpolation on samples every P_in/100; the same on every step (P_in/1,500); and the exact closest approach of the sky-plane motion, |r × v| / |v|. For m₃ = 0.23 M☉, run 9c's way gives a maximum of 0.090 and a median of 0.030 per year (run 9c's REBOUND runs: 0.074 and 0.034), while the exact reading gives 0.037 and 0.026, identical to the fine-step one. The inflation is one-sided, as an artefact should be: run 9c's REBOUND values exceed the exact ones by more than 0.01 in 17 of 48 configurations and fall below them by more than 0.005 in only 3. Its size in any one configuration depends on the sampling phase, which is why the per-configuration agreement was poor (correlation 0.55). For m₃ = 0.864 the readings agree better (exact 0.101/0.081, interpolated 0.099/0.072), because the true slope is larger than the drift.
- **The full scan.** Run 9c's node offsets 0°, 60°, 120°, 180° correspond (for an edge-on inner orbit) to mutual inclinations of 20°, 62°, 118° and 160°, around the fastest range. Scanning in 10° steps (228 configurations, exact reading) the fastest rates are at mutual inclinations of 44–62° and 118–136°: at most **0.038 per year for m₃ = 0.23 M☉** and **0.106 per year for 0.864 M☉** (ratio 2.8), with the slowest near 90° and near coplanar, as secular theory expects.
- **What changes.** Run 9's conclusion about the light tertiary becomes stronger: on Gaia's orbit it can change b by at most 0.04 per year, 3–6 times slower than the switch-on needs, not 1.5–3 times. Run 9c's "even a 1 M☉ tertiary reaches only 0.092 per year" came from the same interpolation; the exact heavy-root value, 0.106, sits just under the required 0.11–0.22. So the heavy root is the Gaia orbit's best case and is marginal: it works only if the switch-on needed the smallest change run 7 allowed. (Larger stars do not help, contrary to run 9c's option iii: the change needed is fixed in units of R₁ + R₂ by the eclipse depths, so larger stars need a larger swing in inclination, and the integrations' rate in those units falls as 1/(R₁ + R₂).) Section 10b's text uses these exact numbers.
- **For the paper.** If run 9c's sentence "at most 0.07 (R₁ + R₂) per year" is quoted anywhere, it should read "at most 0.04". Section 10e changes the R1003 story more than this does.
- **Limits.** No general relativity here (run 9c had it): relativistic apsidal motion at 2.88 d has a ~5,000-yr period and moves the node not at all, so it cannot matter over 6 years. Fixed-step RK4 at P_in/1,500 changes no rate by more than 0.001 at P_in/3,000 (section 10b).

## 10e. R1003's 2026 eclipses are not deepening, which a steady precession cannot do

Script: [`r1003_depth_forecast.py`](r1003_depth_forecast.py) (NumPy). Input: Fable's per-event and per-sector depths from run 7 (`../run7-temperature-and-r1003/fable-r1003/r1003_events.csv` and `r1003_depths.csv`; copies here). Output: `r1003-depth-forecast-output.txt`.

```
python3 r1003_depth_forecast.py ../run7-temperature-and-r1003/fable-r1003/r1003_events.csv \
    ../run7-temperature-and-r1003/fable-r1003/r1003_depths.csv > r1003-depth-forecast-output.txt
```

- **What the switch-on needed.** Two limb-darkened disks (1.1 and 0.9 R☉; secondary/primary 0.53 fixes their brightness ratio; e sin ω ≈ 0, so both eclipses share one impact parameter) reproduce SPOC's 144 ppt primary at b = 0.64 (0.58 if the heavy Gaia tertiary adds 21% third light). Fable's 2023 limits (about 3 ppt) need b > 0.96. So between the last eclipse-free event (May 2023, s65) and the first deep one (February 2026, s99), 2.62 years, b fell by at least 0.33–0.38: **at least 0.125–0.145 (R₁ + R₂) per year on average**. Radius splits from 1.0 : 1.0 to 1.2 : 0.8 R☉ give 0.119–0.135 (0.138–0.157 with the third light), and the deepening the switch-on implies for 2026 (below) stays at 77–80 ppt per year in all of them. This replaces run 7's estimate (0.3–0.6) with a measured lower bound, and puts the heavy Gaia root's best 0.106 per year (10d) 15–27% short.
- **What 2026 shows.** At that rate the primary must deepen by about 80 ppt per year now (dDepth/db ≈ −540 to −640 ppt), about 5 ppt across one sector. It doesn't. SPOC s100's eight eclipses run 146.1, 145.7, 145.0, 143.6, 146.2, 146.1, 145.4, 144.8 ppt: a slope of −7 ± 17 ppt per year. QLP s99 to s101 (54 days apart, same product) goes from 97.2 to 92.1 ppt, the wrong way. Converted, b is changing by **+0.02 ± 0.03 per year** in 2026 against the ≤ −0.125 per year the switch-on needed: about 6σ apart. Even without any geometry, a depth that rose from < 3 to 144 ppt within 2.6 years averaged at least 54 ppt per year, against −7 ± 17 now.
- **What it means.** Any tertiary on Gaia's 206.6-d orbit, light or heavy, precesses the pair over centuries, so its db/dt is constant over a few years: it cannot be fast enough for the switch-on and near zero a year later. The 2026 rate itself (|db/dt| ≲ 0.07 per year, 2σ) is consistent with the light root (≤ 0.038). So either something changes the inclination on a timescale of years rather than centuries (a close perturber; but see the next point), or the eclipses' appearance was not a slow geometric sweep at all. It also weakens 10b: the heavy root no longer "explains" the switch-on, and RVs are now about Gaia's mass, not about the switch-on.
- **The close perturber is squeezed too.** [`r1003_etv_window.py`](r1003_etv_window.py) → `r1003-etv-window-output.txt` fits Fable's 19 deep 2026 eclipse times with a linear ephemeris, the ~83-d wiggle (χ² 465 → 28 for 15 dof; 75–106 d all fit) and a sinusoid at each period in 10b's 8–17-d window. Nothing significant turns up (best 13.8 d, Δχ² = 8 for two parameters over ~180 trial periods), and the 95% amplitude limit is 27–37 s. At leading order that caps a fourth body there at **5–12 Jupiter masses** (0.005–0.011 M☉), well below 10b's "a few hundredths". Such a body precesses the pair with a nodal period of roughly (4/3)(M/m₄)P₄²/P_in, 30–70 years: fast enough on average, but nearly steady over 2023–2026, so it, too, cannot make a fast switch-on followed by a flat 2026 unless b happens to be at its turning point now. So the flat depths point away from any perturber allowed here and toward something non-geometric (or a turning point, which TESS will show as the eclipses starting to fade).
- **What TESS will show next.** On a steady sweep at the switch-on's rate, the next sectors would show the primary about 80 ppt deeper per year, with b reaching 0 within about five years. Flat depths over another sector or two would make "not a steady precession" firm. Durations would be a dilution-free check: the primary's width scales as √(1 − b²) and changes about 1% per 0.01 in b at b ≈ 0.64.
- **Limits.** Radii assumed from the house's 1.1 + 0.9 M☉ pair (other splits above), quadratic limb darkening with fixed coefficients; e sin ω taken as 0 (both eclipses appeared together, which supports it); Fable's per-event depths are template fits with the shape fixed, and their sector-to-sector QLP dilution is assumed constant (Fable's check).
