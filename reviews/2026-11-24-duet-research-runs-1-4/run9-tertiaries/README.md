**Summary.** (9a) Fitting A73's whole secondary-eclipse history with an N-body triple, and then asking which fits Gaia's astrometric wobble allows, gives one answer: a ≈ 0.85 M☉ tertiary that emits little light (a massive white dwarf, or a close pair of low-mass stars), nearly coplanar with the eclipsing pair, on a nearly circular 124.7-d orbit. (9b) Gaia's photocentre orbits weigh the tertiaries: R1003's is 0.23 M☉; A73's must be ≥ 0.86 M☉ and faint. ## 9a. A73: TESS's eclipse history and Gaia's astrometry agree on a 0.85 M☉ dark tertiary, on a rounder orbit than Gaia's fit

Scripts: [`a73_fit.py`](a73_fit.py) (differential-evolution fit), [`a73_polish.py`](a73_polish.py) (Nelder–Mead polish), [`a73_assess.py`](a73_assess.py) (residuals, depth check, forecast, 680-yr stability), [`a73_figure.py`](a73_figure.py) → [`a73-nbody-fit.svg`](a73-nbody-fit.svg). They need `rebound`, NumPy and SciPy. Fits (in `fits/`): `joint_e*.json` and `gaiamass_e*.json` (differential evolution), `pol_e*.json` (all masses allowed) and `gpol_e*.json` (polished; `g` = m₃ held to 0.85–0.95 M☉). Output: [`a73-assess-output.txt`](a73-assess-output.txt). Input: Fable's 24 secondary eclipses, s17–s86, with phases and depths ([`a73-secondary-phases.csv`](a73-secondary-phases.csv), from the house timing table, 2026-11-25).

Run 7 showed by hand that a tertiary on Gaia's 124.7-d orbit can reproduce A73's secondary-phase swing if e_out is about 0.10–0.15 and m₃ ≈ 0.3–0.4 M☉. Fable has since measured every secondary eclipse through s86, and two things changed: the swing turned round in mid-2024 (phase 0.530 in 2021, 0.461 in mid-2024, rising since), and the fade reversed (secondary depth 14.6% in 2019–20, 6.1% in early 2024, 7.9–9.3% by s85–86). That is a full half-cycle with both ends, so it can be fitted rather than eyeballed.

**Method.** A three-body integration (inner 1.2 + 1.0 M☉, R = 1.4 + 1.0 R☉ as in run 7; outer orbit at Gaia's period and astrometric inclination 90.95°), started in 2019 and run through 2028, with every eclipse found as seen from Earth (light-travel time included). Free: the inner e and ω, m₃, the outer ω and phase, and the mutual inclination and its orientation; e_out is held fixed at 0.10, 0.15, … 0.50 in turn. Fit: all 24 secondary phases (floor 0.002) and depths (uniform discs with a free depth scale, floor 0.005 in depth), so the model's impact parameter must follow the fade. Each fit is differential evolution followed by Nelder–Mead. χ² values compare fits; their absolute size reflects the floors I chose.

(The unconstrained fits at e_out = 0.10 and 0.15 already land at 0.87 and 0.84 M☉, inside the Gaia range. Differential evolution is stochastic: two seeds at Gaia's mass and e_out = 0.41 gave 3,202 and 1,617 before polishing, so treat differences of tens of percent as noise and factors of two or more as real.)

- **Two families reproduce the eclipses.** A heavy, nearly coplanar tertiary on a round orbit (m₃ ≈ 0.85 M☉, e_out 0.10–0.15, i_mut ≈ 1.7°) and a light, more inclined one on a moderately eccentric orbit (m₃ ≈ 0.17–0.26 M☉, e_out 0.25–0.30, i_mut ≈ 5–6°). Both match the phase swing to 0.004 (about the eclipse-to-eclipse scatter) and track the fade (Spearman −0.96 between model impact parameter and measured depth), and both are stable for 2,000 outer orbits.
- **Gaia's astrometry picks the heavy family, and matches it.** The heavy fits imply a photocentre orbit of 0.71–0.73 mas if the tertiary is dark; Gaia measured 0.719 mas (section 9b). The light family implies 0.17 mas, four times too small. So two independent measurements, the eclipse timing and fade from TESS and the astrometric wobble from Gaia, give the same tertiary mass, ≈ 0.85 M☉, provided it emits little light: a massive white dwarf, or a close pair of M/K dwarfs.
- **The agreement holds when the inner masses change.** Refitting at e_out = 0.10 and 0.30 with a lighter (1.0 + 0.8 M☉) and a heavier (1.4 + 1.2 M☉) eclipsing pair (`fits/min_*.json`, `M_IN=… JOINT=1 python3 a73_fit.py …`, differential evolution only): the heavy family moves with the inner mass, to m₃ = 0.69 and 0.99 M☉, while the dark-tertiary mass Gaia's 0.719 mas requires moves to 0.76 and 0.95 M☉. So TESS and Gaia agree to within ~10% for all three assumed inner masses, and e_out = 0.10 fits better than 0.30 in both (χ² 436 vs 697 and 236 vs 486).
- **Gaia's eccentricity does not survive.** At e_out = 0.41 the best fit of all masses wants a 0.11 M☉ tertiary (which Gaia's amplitude rules out), misses the fade (depth χ² 1,069) and is torn apart within 3 years. With Gaia's own mass (0.85–0.95 M☉) the fit worsens steadily with e_out: about twice as bad at 0.20–0.25, four times as bad and dynamically unstable (within 3–20 years) at 0.30 and 0.41. Gaia's e = 0.41 ± 0.10 (Fable: GOF 4.55, corr(e, P) = +0.56) is 2.7σ from 0.15 and 3.2σ from 0.10, which is what the astrometric fit's known problems (single photocentre orbit, unmodelled inner wobble) allow.
- **A prediction.** The heavy family forecasts the secondary phase climbing back to ~0.53–0.54 by 2027–28 and the eclipses deepening again (impact parameter back near 0.1 of R₁ + R₂, so depths like 2019–21), see [`a73-nbody-fit.svg`](a73-nbody-fit.svg). Any new TESS sector at this position tests it; the next DR4 epoch astrometry tests e_out directly.
- **For the paper.** Replace run 7's suggested wording with: "An N-body model with Gaia's 124.7-d orbit as the tertiary reproduces A73's secondary-phase swing (including its 2024 reversal) and the fade and recovery of its eclipses for a 0.85 M☉ tertiary, nearly coplanar (i_mut ≈ 2°) on a nearly circular orbit (e ≈ 0.1–0.15). That mass matches Gaia's photocentre amplitude if the tertiary is dark, e.g. a white dwarf. Gaia's e = 0.41 is unstable for this system; DR4 should find a lower value."
- **Limits.** Radii of the inner pair are assumed (masses were varied, above); the depth model is uniform discs; the search is a heuristic optimisation in seven dimensions, so another family may exist that I did not find; the heavy family's χ² is about twice the light family's (mostly in depth, where uniform discs are crude). The conclusion rests on Gaia's a₀ selecting between families, not on χ² alone.

## 9b. What Gaia's photocentre orbits say about the tertiaries' masses

Script: [`tertiary_mass.py`](tertiary_mass.py) (NumPy, SciPy). Input: Fable's ESA Gaia TAP copy of both orbits, [`nss.csv`](nss.csv) (2026-11-25). Output: [`tertiary-mass-output.txt`](tertiary-mass-output.txt).

Run 7 had to assume the tertiary masses. Fable's full Gaia solutions (answers to run 7's asks 1 and 2) include the Thiele–Innes constants, so the size of the photocentre orbit, a₀, is measured. For an eclipsing pair of mass M₁₂ orbited by a tertiary m₃, a₀ = a·(m₃/(M₁₂ + m₃) − β), where a is the relative orbit (Kepler) and β the tertiary's share of the G-band light. A dark tertiary gives the minimum m₃; a main-sequence tertiary (here L ∝ m⁴) needs more, and beyond a point it gets bright enough to shrink the photocentre orbit again, so there is a largest a₀ a main-sequence tertiary can produce.

- **R1003: m₃ = 0.23 M☉ (68%: 0.22–0.24).** a₀ = 0.817 mas = 0.091 AU at 8.98 mas parallax, photocentric mass function 0.0024 M☉. For a 1.1 + 0.9 M☉ inner pair the tertiary is an M dwarf of 0.23 M☉, whether dark, main-sequence or itself a pair; it contributes almost no light, so this mass is robust. Run 7 tested m₃ = 0.25–1 M☉ and leaned on the 1 M☉ case; the measured mass is at the bottom of that range (section 9c).
- **A73: the orbit is too big for an ordinary tertiary.** a₀ = 0.719 mas = 0.199 AU, mass function 0.067 M☉. A dark tertiary must be at least 0.86 M☉ (68%: 0.75–0.98); a single main-sequence tertiary can produce at most 0.435 mas (at m₃ ≈ 0.63 M☉), because a heavier one is bright enough to pull the photocentre back. So with a 1.2 + 1.0 M☉ eclipsing pair, Gaia's 0.72 mas needs a massive, faint companion: a white dwarf of ≳ 0.86 M☉, or a pair of M/K dwarfs totalling ≈ 1.0 M☉ (their light is small: two 0.5 M☉ dwarfs give β ≈ 4%). Either is possible, but both differ from run 7's N-body fit, which reproduced TESS with m₃ ≈ 0.3–0.4 M☉ (that would give a₀ ≈ 0.28–0.36 mas, half of what Gaia measured).
- **And a heavier tertiary makes Gaia's e_out = 0.41 harder, not easier.** Fable's numbers put Gaia's e = 0.412 ± 0.097 (errors scaled for GOF 4.55), with corr(e, P) = +0.56. Section 9a tests stability and the TESS phase history at m₃ ≈ 0.9 M☉.
- **Three readings, for the paper to choose between** (or leave open, as it does now): (i) the tertiary is a massive white dwarf or a close M-dwarf pair, and the true e_out is near the low end of Gaia's range; (ii) Gaia's a₀ and e are inflated by the inner pair's own photocentre wobble and other unmodelled signal (GOF 4.55; the inner 17.5-d pair moves the photocentre by up to ~0.03 AU, too little to explain a factor of two by itself); (iii) the house's assumed inner masses are too low. DR4 epoch astrometry (December) decides (ii) directly.
- **For the paper.** A sentence for A73: "Gaia's photocentre orbit (a₀ = 0.72 mas) requires a faint tertiary of at least 0.86 M☉, e.g. a white dwarf, or an unresolved pair of low-mass stars; a single main-sequence tertiary would produce at most 0.44 mas." And for R1003: "The Gaia photocentre orbit implies a 0.23 M☉ tertiary."

## 9c. R1003: the measured Gaia orbit is too weak to switch the eclipses on

Scripts: [`r1003_switchon.py`](r1003_switchon.py) and [`r1003_switchon_robust.py`](r1003_switchon_robust.py) (need `rebound`, `reboundx`, NumPy; general relativity on via REBOUNDx `gr_full`, which answers Fable's ask 4 in part). Outputs: [`r1003-switchon-output.txt`](r1003-switchon-output.txt), [`r1003-robust-output.txt`](r1003-robust-output.txt).

Run 7 left R1003 (2.88-d eclipses that appeared between 2023 and 2026, inside Gaia's 206.6-d astrometric orbit) with an assumed e_out = 0.3, an edge-on outer orbit and tertiary masses of 0.25–1 M☉; it found that only the 1 M☉ case came close to the required switch-on rate. Fable's full Gaia solution now fixes all three:

- **The outer orbit is eccentric and inclined:** e_out = 0.491 ± 0.045, astrometric inclination 70.3° (68.8–71.8°). An eclipsing inner orbit has i_in between about 79° and 101° (b < 1 for R₁ + R₂ ≈ 2 R☉ at a ≈ 10.7 R☉), so the mutual inclination is at least ~9°, and at least 19.7° if the inner orbit is exactly edge-on (Fable's ≳ 20° is the edge-on case). Nodal precession therefore must swing the inner orbit's sky inclination by many degrees over a cycle, more than the ~11° eclipse window. So the eclipses switching on and off over the long run is guaranteed.
- **But the tertiary is light: m₃ = 0.23 M☉ (section 9b), and that makes the swing slow.** With the measured orbit, m₃ = 0.23 M☉ and GR included, the impact parameter changes by at most 0.074 (R₁ + R₂) per year over any 2.7-yr window, in 48 configurations (inner sky inclination 84°, 90°, 96°; four node offsets; two outer arguments of periastron; two outer phases; median 0.034 per year). The mutual-inclination scan (19.7° to 118°) gives at most 0.032 per year. Even a 1 M☉ tertiary on this orbit reaches only 0.092 per year. Run 7 estimated that the switch-on needs b to fall by 0.3–0.6 in ≤ 2.7 years, i.e. 0.11–0.22 per year: 1.5–3 times faster than the fastest configuration found here, and 3–6 times the median.
- **GR does not quench the third body.** With GR on, the inner eccentricity still swings (e_in 0.31–0.48 within 12 years at a 90° mutual inclination), as expected: relativistic apsidal motion at 2.88 d has a period of ~5,000 yr and the tidal one a few hundred years, both slower than the tertiary's Kozai–Lidov timescale (~260 yr for m₃ = 0.23 M☉, e_out = 0.49). Tides were not added (the inner pair's tidal Love numbers are unknown); they would add apsidal motion that damps the eccentricity cycles, and would not speed up the nodal swing that moves the eclipses.
- **So what turned R1003's eclipses on?** Either (i) a fourth body on a closer orbit (run 7's hypothetical ~80-d tertiary at 20–40° gives 0.2–0.5 per year), which Gaia's single-companion solution would not show if it is faint and close; (ii) the 2023–2026 "switch-on" is partly a coverage or dilution effect (the house's flat earlier sectors and its product controls argue against this, but it is the cheaper hypothesis to rule out); or (iii) the inner pair's radii are larger than assumed (an evolved primary widens the window and lowers the required rate in proportion). Gaia DR4's epoch astrometry (December) can test (i): a second periodicity in the residuals of the 206.6-d orbit.
- **For the paper.** Suggested wording for R1003: "Gaia's astrometric orbit (P = 206.6 d, e = 0.49, i = 70.3°) implies a 0.23 M☉ tertiary inclined by at least 9° to the eclipsing pair; it will precess the pair's orbit through and out of eclipse over centuries, but N-body integrations (with general relativity) give at most 0.07 (R₁ + R₂) per year of impact-parameter change, too slow for the eclipses' appearance within three years. A further, closer companion is the most likely cause."

## 9d. R1117: a drifting depth ratio means the stars changed, or the orbit is (slightly) eccentric and moving

Script: [`r1117_geometry.py`](r1117_geometry.py) (NumPy only). Input: Fable's per-sector depths, [`r1117-per-sector.csv`](r1117-per-sector.csv) (house QLP/SPOC cache, 27 sectors 2020–2025, made 2026-11-25). Output: [`r1117-geometry-output.txt`](r1117-geometry-output.txt).

Fable confirmed errata's period doubling (B shallower in 27 of 27 sectors) and found that the depth ratio B/A falls from 0.92–0.95 (2020–21) to 0.79–0.86 (2025). The question is what physics can move that ratio, because the usual suspect, a slowly changing inclination, cannot.

- **In a circular orbit the depth ratio barely depends on inclination.** Both conjunctions then happen at the same sky separation, so the companion covers the same lens-shaped area of each star; the ratio is just the two stars' surface-brightness ratio, weighted by limb darkening over the covered patch. In a toy grazing model (1.0 + 0.9 R☉, P = 2.6367 d, linear limb darkening), sweeping the inclination from 80° to 85.8° takes the depths from 15 to 290 ppt while B/A moves only from 0.809 to 0.826, i.e. by 2%. Dilution also cancels in the ratio. So the observed fall of 12% (core B/A 0.924 → 0.808; trend −0.025 per year, rms 0.02 about it in every era) is not a geometry-of-inclination effect and not a contamination effect.
- **What can do it.** (1) A change in the surface-brightness ratio of the eclipsed patches. In a synchronised 2.6-d binary a grazing eclipse covers the same patch of each star every orbit, at one latitude near the limb, so a spot belt or active region growing there over an activity cycle changes one depth and not the other. The release's own visual note ("dips sit on a smoothly varying raw-flux baseline") says the stars are spotted. (2) An eccentric orbit whose e sin ω changes: the two conjunctions then sit at different separations, and in a grazing eclipse depth is steep in separation. At ~80 ppt depth the model needs Δ(e sin ω) ≈ 0.014 to drop B/A by 12% (0.005 gives 4%, 0.010 gives 8.5%). Tidal and relativistic apsidal motion at 2.64 d is far too slow for that (apsidal period of order 10³ yr, so Δω ≲ 1° in five years, which would need e ≳ 0.8); only a third body forcing the eccentricity could, and there is no Gaia orbit for this star in the repository.
- **One hint that is not decisive.** In the one product where dilution should be stable, SPOC two-minute data, A deepened (86.8 → 95.4 ppt) while B got shallower (80.2 → 76.8) between 2020–21 and s97 (2025). Opposite moves are what a changing e sin ω does (and what spots on both stars can do); an inclination change moves both the same way. It is a single 2025 SPOC sector, so this is a pointer, not a result.
- **How the house can tell the two apart with data it already has.** Per sector: (a) the time of B relative to A (phase − 0.5): a forced eccentricity that changes e sin ω by ~0.01 will in general change e cos ω by a similar amount, and that moves B by 2P/π × Δ(e cos ω) ≈ 24 min per 0.01, easily measured at 2-min cadence (a constant offset from 0.5 would also show that e ≠ 0); (b) the A and B durations: the model gives T14 2.64 h against 2.55 h at e sin ω = 0.015 (about 3.5%); (c) the out-of-eclipse spot-modulation amplitude per sector: if the ratio tracks the modulation, it is spots. A timing shift and duration split would point to a third body; neither, plus a correlation with modulation, points to spots.
- **For the paper.** R1117's row should read P = 2.636664 d (twin solution with unequal eclipses, as errata and Fable found), and the drift belongs in a sentence only after (a)–(c): "the depth ratio of the two eclipses decreased by ~12% between 2020 and 2025; [spot evolution / a changing eccentricity] is the likely cause."
