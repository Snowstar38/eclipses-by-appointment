# Duet's findings on the star paper

Current findings from checks of the release, grouped by topic. Each entry gives the claim, the key numbers, a status (solid, tentative or open) and the files. Proposed table and text changes are collected, one row per change, in [`ledger.csv`](ledger.csv) (built by `ledger.py`; status proposed, favoured, open or for decision). Nothing here edits another table.

Run any script from the repository root, for example `python3 reviews/duet-findings/host-table/nov23_columns.py`; scripts that take arguments say so in their first lines. They need Python 3 with numpy and pandas (Pillow for one figure). Noted exceptions need SciPy, Astropy or rebound, or files kept in the house folder. Every number comes from files in this repository; this environment cannot reach MAST, VizieR, SIMBAD or the Gaia archive.

- **R1117 is a near-twin at 2.636664 d with steady eclipses.** Measured against their own flanks, eclipse B is 2.0 ± 0.4 ppt (2.4%) shallower than A, in 25 of 27 sectors, with era-median B/A 0.967, 0.977, 0.989 (trend +0.005 ± 0.002 per year). ## Gaia errors and the appointment windows

- **About one Gaia DR3 period in twenty misses by 3σ.** Counted without tier, 7 of 199 R systems; 12 of 184 in the A sample. Counting confirmed systems only is circular, since a failed Gaia check was a reason to grade probable. Solid. `gaia-errors/gaia_pulls.py`, `tier_reasons.py`.
- **Gaia's SB2 errors leave out their own poor fit; scaled by it, they are right.** As published, 8 of 29 same-period SB2s against 5 of 155 SB1s miss the eclipse period by 3σ (p = 1e-4; robust width 2.31). The SB2 fits are poor (median goodness of fit 7.5, up to 28.8; √(χ²/ν) median 2.1, 0.91–5.3), and dividing each pull by its own √(χ²/ν) brings the period pulls to width 1.12 with none beyond 3σ, and the conjunction pulls at Gaia's epoch from width 2.95 (10 beyond 3σ) to 1.26 (3): A29 (−6.2σ; its conjunction B misses too), A167 (+4.3σ; a third body in its timing) and A89 (+3.5σ). Solid. `gaia-errors/sb1_vs_sb2_periods.py`, `fit_quality_scaling.py`.
- **Gaia's correlated conjunction errors run small.** On the 184 same-period A systems, at Gaia's epoch, the published correlations are 1.3–1.7× too small (about 3× for SB2s); independent errors are about 5× too big. Windows from the correlations at 3σ would miss 11 of the 184 found eclipses, 7 of them Table 4 orbits. The search's windows match a 4.5–6σ correlated window (97–108% of its total width), about as wide as needed, so they should not be narrowed. On the R sample, full-covariance times are close to calibrated (robust width 0.70 over 476 measured epochs; 1.29 at Gaia's own epoch over 167). Solid. `gaia-errors/a_epoch_test.py`, `search_window_budget.py`, `window_covariance.py`, `gaia_epoch_test.py`; Fable 5.1's `fable51-*.csv`.
- **Gaia's eccentricity errors are also about 1.4× too small, and near-circular fits run high.** At P < 3 d, where the eclipses show the orbits are circular, the free-eccentricity SB1/SB2 fits give e/σ with a Rayleigh scale of 1.37 ± 0.12 (31 systems; 1.44 ± 0.17 for 17 below 2.5 d) instead of 1; R467 and R607 are above 3σ, and R607's e = 0.32 ± 0.05 at 2.13 d, with no secondary measured, looks like a failed fit like R1819's. For 14 A systems whose secondary phase the paper states, Gaia's |e cos ω| against the phase's gives rms pulls of 1.35 (1.80 with A145, whose Gaia e = 0.038 ± 0.008 is 4.7σ from a circular orbit at phase 0.49996), and 11 of the 14 pulls are positive. The two clearly eccentric ones agree closely (A123 0.224 against 0.226, A151 0.098 against 0.093). The same factor as the conjunction-time errors. Tentative (small samples). `gaia-errors/eccentricity_errors.py`.
- **Most of Table 4's e cos ω contradictions are what Gaia's understated errors produce by chance.** With eccentricity errors 1.37 ± 0.12 times the quoted ones, 184 compared orbits should give 5.2 (2.9–8.1) chance 3σ failures in e cos ω; Table 4 has 8. Scaled by each SB1's own goodness of fit (next entry), A21, A63, A159 and A202 (F2 1.8–2.8) fall to 1.7–2.2σ (the paper's A159 note already suspects understated errors from its goodness of fit of 2.6), while A145 (F2 −1.27) stays at 4.6σ and A65 (F2 −0.53) at 3.6σ: those two are well-fitted solutions that the eclipses contradict, and both also miss their conjunctions at Gaia's epoch (3.3σ and 3.1σ after scaling). A28 and A30 are SB2s, with goodness of fit 14.9 and 23.7, so their contradictions sit on poorly fitted solutions (with a flat 1.37: 4.2σ and 3.9σ). The period failures are different: the A sample's period pulls have a robust width of 1.16 and do not follow the goodness of fit, so 1.8 chance failures against 13 observed is a real excess, though for the R systems tested the orbit is still the right binary (next entries). Tentative. `gaia-errors/table4_scaled.py`, `fit_quality_scaling.py`.
- **Gaia's SB1 errors are right for well-fitted orbits and grow with the goodness of fit.** Conjunction pulls at Gaia's epoch (full covariance) widen with the solution's F2: robust width 1.05, 1.30 and 2.22 across its terciles for 155 A systems (Spearman p = 0.001), and 1.22, 0.93 and 1.76 for 165 R systems (p = 0.007). It holds within confirmed systems alone (273; widths 1.05, 1.23, 1.88, p = 1e-4), so it is not the tier grading, which used Gaia tension. Over the 320 together the errors need a scale of 1.24 exp(0.18 F2) (slope 0.10–0.22 at 68%, never zero in 300 bootstraps): 1.04× at F2 = −1, 1.5× at 1, 2.1× at 3, steeper than the solutions' own √(χ²/ν), which reaches only about 1.5. It calibrates every F2 quartile (widths 0.86–1.09, against 0.76–1.47 for a flat 1.44× of the same mean). Fitted on conjunctions alone, it also fixes the eccentricity errors: on 30 circular SB1s below 3 d the Rayleigh scale of e/σ is 0.93 below the median F2 and 1.73 above, 0.95 overall once scaled. So the average factors of the entries above (1.3–1.7× on conjunctions, 1.37× on e) are a mix of right errors on good fits and errors about twice too small near F2 = 3, where DR3's SB1 cut stops (Triples). At the same total window width the scale leaves as many 3σ conjunction misses (4 of 320) as a flat factor; those that remain have low F2 (A41, A65, A145, P025), so they mark wrong orbits, not noise. Gaia's period pulls do not follow F2 (width 0.96–1.08 across terciles). Among the accepted R systems' SB1s, F2 runs higher for cooler stars (Spearman −0.21 with Teff, p = 1e-5) and larger semi-amplitudes (+0.15), and does not follow brightness, RUWE, period or eccentricity. Nor does F2 mark the systems whose primary falls on the other conjunction (kind-1 fraction 0.08–0.14 in every F2 bin, 471 SB1s with clean timing) or the probable tier (median 0.78 against 0.88 for confirmed). SB2s are treated above. F2 and obj_func fix the degrees of freedom, which recovers N_RV where an export has placeholders (exact on all 816 SB1 rows that list it). Solid for the trend, tentative for the exact law. `gaia-errors/fit_quality_scaling.py` (SciPy for its last block), `fit-quality-scaling.csv`, `fit-quality-scaling-output.txt`.
- **R716, R752, R787 and R789 are contradicted Gaia orbits.** Carried back to Gaia's epoch their eclipses land within 1–5 h of Gaia's conjunctions (R814 too; chance about 10⁻⁹ for all five), but their Gaia periods are 3σ off: right binary, wrong Gaia period. Table 4 would have 24. R810 does not fit; R771 is undecided. Solid. `gaia-errors/gaia_epoch_test.py`.
- **A29's conjunction B also misses at Gaia's epoch (1.7 d).** Solid. `gaia-errors/a_epoch_test.py`.
- **R systems held at probable for conjunction tension** (R740, R816, R723, R755, R833) carry no more of it than a dozen confirmed A systems, and with Gaia's errors scaled by each orbit's goodness of fit all five are within 2σ at Gaia's epoch (R833 +1.9, R816 +1.6, R740 −1.6, R755 +1.3, R723 +0.1). For decision. `gaia-errors/r_tension_vs_a.py`, `fit_quality_scaling.py`.
- **R729's Gaia SB2 orbit is wrong.** Its eclipses need e ≥ 0.50 like Gaia's but miss its conjunctions and ω, and its semi-amplitudes give 0.30 + 0.31 M☉ for a 5,800 K, 4.2 L☉ pair (TIC mass 1.05 M☉). Solid. `gaia-errors/r729_epoch.py` (Fable 5.1's event times), `masses/sb2_masses.py`.
- **GD1 and GD2** fold cleanly onto Gaia's conjunction A with no odd/even difference. Solid. `gaia-errors/gd_fold.py` (light curves are in the house folder, not here).

## Gaia geometry

- **Gaia's ω predicts which eclipse TESS sees.** In 438 of 491 systems with unambiguous timing the primary falls on the conjunction where Gaia's visible star is eclipsed. Exceptions are evolved visible stars, near-equal companions (2% rising to 71% with mass and radius ratio), or eccentric orbits eclipsing near periastron only. About 15% (68% range 5–26%) of the probable Gaia-fold tier look mis-associated. Gaia eccentricities below about 0.1 under 8 d are mostly noise. Solid. `gaia-geometry/conjunction_kind.py`, `durations.py`, `misassociation.py`; 28 systems to look at in `kind0-check-list.csv`.
- **Gaia's astrometric orbits pass the eclipse test.** Four accepted systems eclipse on an orbit Gaia DR3 solved astrometrically: A19 (290.9 d), A72 (215.0 d), A74 (173.9 d) and A20 (26.38 d, a validated targeted-search solution beside its SB1). An eclipse needs the inclination within 1.2–1.7° of 90° on the three long orbits (7° for A20, a subgiant); Gaia's inclinations are 91.2 ± 2.4°, 91.8 ± 3.0°, 90.3 ± 1.6° and 89.7 ± 0.8°. With the periods and the conjunction times (full covariance) against the eclipse ephemerides, the 12 pulls have an rms of 0.91, so Gaia's astrometric errors look right, unlike its spectroscopic conjunction and eccentricity errors. A20's astrometric orbit has its SB1's size (a0 0.0316 au against a1 sin i 0.0304 au) but a lower eccentricity, 0.142 ± 0.030 against 0.221 ± 0.003. A5's validated targeted-search solution is near-circular with Thiele–Innes errors 225 times the orbit, so it tests nothing; its period is 3.0σ from the eclipses, where Gaia's SB1 agrees. Tentative (four systems). `gaia-geometry/astrometric_eclipsers.py`, `astrometric-eclipsers.csv`, `astrometric-eclipsers-output.txt`.
- **Six R-system Gaia orbits cannot host their eclipses.** Of 683 accepted systems with both periods, 667 match (664 directly, 3 at a harmonic). Of the 16 that don't, the paper already treats A45, A54 and A113 as wrong Gaia periods. Of the 13 R systems, R732, R753, R757, R788, R792 and R798 cannot be the inner pair of the Gaia orbit (Mardling–Aarseth); R731, R739, R778, R785, R790, R969 and R1003 are allowed triples. Solid. `gaia-geometry/gaia_vs_eclipse.py`, `gaia_mismatch_stability.py`.
- **Companion size supports the association.** For 463 Gaia-fold systems with a usable orbit and depth, Gaia's mass function allows a companion able to make the eclipse, bar one marginal giant; it cannot pick out the probable tier's blends. Solid. `gaia-geometry/companion_size.py`.
- **R490** has the sample's largest mass function (1.29 M☉) and a primary on the companion's conjunction: probably a giant with a hot, B-type companion. Tentative; a spectroscopy target.
- **Unconfirmed single dips near the ecliptic**: "plausible" verdicts are twice as common within 20° (13 of 36 against 56 of 344), but only 1.8σ once coverage is allowed for; 14 signals listed for a moving-object check. Tentative. `gaia-geometry/ecliptic_plausible.py` (needs SciPy and Astropy), `near-ecliptic-plausible.csv`.

## Tidal circularisation

- **Short binaries are circular.** None of 94 secondaries below 2 d has |e cos ω| > 0.031; the eccentric fraction reaches about 70% beyond 12 d; the envelope opens near 3 d. The paper has no circularisation discussion. Solid. `circularisation/circularisation.py`, `ecosw-vs-period.svg`.
- **Hot binaries stay eccentric closer in.** At 4 ≤ a/R₁ < 10, stars at or above 6,250 K are eccentric in 10 of 46 cases against 5 of 178 cool ones (p = 7e-5; 7 of 46, p = 0.0035, without doubtful secondaries); the midpoint moves from about 9.1 to 4.2 d (about 2σ). Tentative. `circularisation/temperature_split.py` (Fable 5's temperature join).

## Triples

- **A73 has a heavy, dark tertiary on a near-circular orbit.** Gaia's e_out = 0.41 is unstable within years. Fits of the whole secondary-eclipse history with e_out 0.10–0.15, a mutual inclination of a few degrees and Gaia's photocentre amplitude (0.719 mas) give m₃ ≈ 0.85 M☉, faint (a white dwarf or a close low-mass pair), and reproduce the phase swing, single-cycle periods and fade. TESS next looks in sectors 123–124 (about August–September 2028): the heavy fits predict a secondary of 121–130 ppt at phase 0.51–0.52, the light ones 86–107 ppt, so the depth decides. Tentative. `triples/a73_fit.py`, `a73_polish.py`, `a73_assess.py`, `a73_forecast.py`, `tertiary_mass.py` (rebound and SciPy for the fits and forecast).
- **R1003's eclipses switched on in 2023–26, faster than its tertiary can explain.** The table row averages over eclipse-free sectors (epoch in s65; tabled P 21σ off the 2026 P = 2.878843 d, with errors scaled by the timing scatter; tabled depth a quarter of 2026's 144 ppt). The 2026 P takes the s101 clock fault out (see Timing); a sector-to-sector scatter of about 30 s remains between the QLP s99 and s101 and SPOC s100 times (Fable 5's own repair gives P = 2.878858 d and −27/+11/−5 s). Gaia's photocentre orbit (206.6 d) allows a 0.23 or a 0.86 M☉ tertiary. The impact parameter must have fallen by ≥ 0.33–0.38 (R₁+R₂) from May 2023 to February 2026, ≥ 0.125 per year, but those tertiaries manage at most 0.038 and 0.106 per year. Through 2026 the depths are flat (−7 ± 17 ppt per year where a steady sweep needs about +80). The 2026 timings cap a fourth body at 8–17 d to 7–14 Jupiter masses; the slow timing wiggle is no larger than that sector-to-sector scatter. So the cause is non-geometric, or b is turning round now; the next TESS sectors and RVs (systemic semi-amplitude about 17 against 5 km/s) decide. Solid for the numbers, open for the cause. `triples/r1003_triple.py`, `r1003_heavy_root.py`, `r1003_node_scan.py`, `r1003_nbody_numpy.py`, `r1003_depth_forecast.py`, `r1003_etv_window.py` (on `fable-r1003/r1003_events_clock_corrected.csv` from `timing/r1003_corrected_events.py`; output `r1003-etv-window-output.txt`), `fourth_body_window.py`, `timing/qlp_barycentre.py`; Fable 5's measurements in `triples/fable-r1003/`.
- **R755's eclipses switched on between 2023 and 2025, and Gaia's tilted outer orbit can do it.** No deep eclipse at covered conjunctions in sectors 8, 9 and 62, then a 313 ppt eclipse in sector 89, two years later. Gaia's astrometric 300.8 d orbit (e = 0.26) is inclined at 66.9° (95%: 49–83°) while the 24.68 d pair eclipses within 2.6° of 90°, so the mutual inclination is at least 20° (5° at the 95% edge). With m₁ = 1.88 M☉ (TIC), m₂ ≥ 0.78 M☉ (Gaia's SB1) and a dark tertiary of ≥ 0.59 M☉ (photocentre orbit), the nodes precess in 45–135 years and the inner orbit crosses the eclipse band at 0.8–3.9° per year for mutual inclinations of 12–60°; the switch-on needs ≥ 0.5° per year. An eclipse epoch then lasts about 1.5–7 years, so the depth should change measurably by sectors 110–111. Third-body timing wander is hours, and a prediction from two eclipses misses by a median 8–25 h after about two years (up to 66 h for a 1 M☉ tertiary), so the paper's ±0.05 d windows should be about ±1 d. Tentative. `triples/r755_precession.py`, `r755-precession-output.txt`, `triple_nbody.py`, `r755_prediction_miss.py` (rebound), `r755-prediction-miss-output.txt`. R1000 and R1549 are the best other November 23 rows for timing variations (`triples/tertiary_orbits.py`).
- **A95's eclipse timing follows Gaia's 541-d astrometric orbit in phase as well as period.** Fable 5's 66 eclipse times (`triples/a95-events.csv`; sectors 16, 22, 23, 49, 50, 76, 77, none affected by the clock faults) fitted with Gaia's P3, e, periastron time and ω held fixed and only the amplitude free give 3.48 ± 0.25 min (primaries 3.24 ± 0.47, secondaries 3.64 ± 0.28, the same sign), cutting χ² from 261.5 to 61.6 with one parameter. Freeing the phase gains only Δχ² = 6.7 and moves the maximum delay by 13 d (9°). The amplitude matches the photocentre lower bound of 3.25 min (68%: 3.01–3.54), so the tertiary adds little light, and the paper's 3.4 ± 0.3 min stands. The same orbit moves the systemic velocity by 8.5 ± 0.6 km/s (semi-amplitude), highest in 2017.1 and 2018.6 and lowest in 2016.5, 2017.9 and 2019.4, which Gaia DR4's epoch RVs should show. Solid. `triples/a95_ltte.py`, `a95-ltte-output.txt`, `dr4_gamma_predictions.py`.
- **A119 is a triple, and TESS timing cannot see it.** Besides its 6.348-d SB2 (the eclipsing near-twins), Gaia fits a 601.2-d astrometric orbit (e = 0.54, i = 106°, significance 16.6), which the paper does not mention. The photocentre orbit gives a light-travel semi-amplitude of at least 3.4 min (68%: 2.9–3.9); the same calculation on A95's 541-d orbit gives 3.25 min against its measured 3.48 ± 0.25. Earthshine's 44 eclipse times in eight sectors (QLP 18, 19 and 52, TESS-SPOC 58–86; QLP 25 unusable, QLP 78 off by background) give −122 ± 96 s (SAP) or +109 ± 105 s (PDCSAP) at Gaia's orbit against ≥ 227 s, so the sign follows the flux column and both zero and the bound are within about 2σ. The same orbit moves the SB2's systemic velocity by a semi-amplitude of at least 9.8 km/s, with extremes in 2015.4 and 2019.9 (sign unknown). Gaia DR3's SB2 holds the systemic velocity fixed and fits poorly: goodness of fit 23.5, against a median of 7.3 for the sample's 38 SB2 solutions, next to A95's 23.1, whose 8.5 km/s swing the timing fixes. Gaia DR4's epoch RVs test it. Tentative. `triples/ltte_from_astrometry.py`, `dr4_gamma_predictions.py` and their outputs; Earthshine's times are in the house folder (`2026-11-26-opus55-a119`).
- **R623 has a tertiary on an eccentric orbit of 5.5 years or more.** Earthshine's 160 TESS-SPOC eclipse times (sectors 15–78, median error 8 s; `timing/r623-events-earthshine.csv`) swing by ±5 min with primaries and secondaries together, so this is light travel and not apsidal motion. An eccentric light-travel orbit fits all of them (χ² 165 for 151, against 256,000 for linear ephemerides), but P3 is bounded below only: ≥ 2,000 d (χ² 174 at 1,800), e ≈ 0.5–0.6, semi-amplitude ≥ 350 s. Two branches fit equally: 2,000–2,500 d with periastron in 2025–26 (M3 ≥ 0.29–0.35 M☉ for a 0.64 + 0.41 M☉ inner pair) and ≥ 3,500 d (M3 ≥ 0.50, and ≥ 0.72 beyond 5,000 d). Three checks:
  - **Gaia's conjunction.** At Gaia's own epoch (2016, ±13 min with the full covariance) Gaia's conjunction agrees with the linear TESS ephemeris (−17.6 min, 1.3σ) and with every light-travel branch (within 1.7σ; 3,000–4,000 d within 0.8σ), so it does not test the tertiary. The offset the review retained (0.064 d at BTJD 2500, against a 0.020 d propagated error) is Gaia's period, 2.3σ short of the TESS-SPOC period (2.0σ against the sample's period-pull width of 1.16; see Gaia errors), carried over 875 cycles. Every branch's apparent 2014–17 period leaves Gaia's 1.5–2.8σ short as well, so Gaia DR3 picks no branch.
  - **Light.** The depths (338 and 99 ppt) allow third light of at most 0.30 of the primary's for main-sequence radii (k = 0.65), and 0.9 even for k = 0.8, so a main-sequence tertiary is at most about 0.4 M☉ (0.6 at the extreme). The short branch fits an M dwarf; the long one (M3 ≥ 0.50) needs a white dwarf or an inflated secondary, and beyond about 5,000 d (M3 ≥ 0.72) a white dwarf in any case.
  - **What decides.** The branches part by about 33 min in the 2027 sectors 121–123 (Earthshine: primary near BTJD 4700.200 short, 4700.178 long) and in the systemic velocity Gaia DR4's epoch RVs will show: the short branch falls by 1–3 km/s from 2016 to 2018 and turns up only in late 2019, the long one rises by 10–13 km/s by 2019. Gaia DR3 also flags an astrometric solution (non-single-star flag 3, RUWE 3.0) that is not in this repository; at a parallax of 7.8 mas the outer orbit accelerates the photocentre by about 1–3 mas yr⁻², so that solution constrains the branch too. ASAS-SN seasons favour the short branch only if their V and g times share a zero point (Earthshine); on a control eclipsing binary with flat TESS timing, ASAS-SN g seasons run 7.0 ± 1.1 min early and scatter 3× beyond their errors, so minute-level ASAS-SN timing needs a control.
  Favoured for the tertiary, open for the branch. `timing/r623_gaia_branches.py`, `r623-gaia-branches.csv`, `r623_third_light.py` and their outputs.
- **Gaia DR3's single-lined orbits cannot carry a fast-moving systemic velocity, so the SB1 input misses the compact, massive triples.** Every Gaia SB1 row in the repository has goodness of fit F2 below 3 (1,312 rows across the review `gaia.json` files and NSS exports; of the 571 in `gaia.json`, 42 are in 2.5–3 and the median is 0.80), while SB2 rows reach 42 (77 of 87 above 3; A95 23.1, A119 23.5) and AstroSpectroSB1 and astrometric orbits also pass 3. The paper applies no fit-quality cut, so DR3 published an SB1 only below F2 = 3. F2 recomputed from `obj_func` with N_RV − 6 degrees of freedom matches every published value, so the cut is on χ² for a constant systemic velocity. Simulated DR3 RVs (the inner orbit plus the tertiary's systemic velocity, random paired epochs over 2014.6–2017.4, per-epoch error from the K₁ error, noise scaled to the sample's median F2) lose half of all SB1s once the drift over the DR3 span reaches about one per-epoch error rms (1.35σ at N_RV 14, 0.85σ at 42); the sample's median is 3.7 km/s (16–84%: 2.3–6.0) at N_RV 22. For a 2 M☉ inner pair on a coplanar circular outer orbit, the chance Gaia publishes the SB1 is 13% for a 0.5 M☉ tertiary at 1 yr, 43% at 2 yr and 70% at 4 yr, and 0%, 2% and 15% for 1 M☉, where light-travel amplitudes are 2–10 min; light (0.1 M☉) or slow (16 yr) tertiaries pass at 95–99%. A95 and A119, with their outer orbits, would be published as SB1s in only 9% and 14% of samplings; they reached the sample as SB2s, which have no such cut. The SB1 light-travel triples have the slow, light outer orbits that pass: published F2 0.64 for R630 (8–40% of simulations at its allowed outer periods go that low), 1.48 for R423 (1–65%) and 2.40 for R623, which only 6% of fits without a tertiary reach; R623's tertiary gives it in 19–50% of simulations at 1,800–2,200 d against 7–8% at 3,000–4,000 d, a mild preference for the short branch. R371's timing allows an island of outer periods near 550–585 d besides ≥ 1,913 d; on the island the systemic velocity would drift by 11 km/s rms over DR3 and Gaia would publish the SB1 in 1% of simulations, so R371's outer period, if it is a light-travel orbit, is at least about 1,900 d (5.2 yr). As a group, the 11 timing-variation candidates with a Gaia SB1 do not fit worse than the 260 timed systems without a timing variation (one-sided rank p = 0.27; the three light-travel systems alone 0.14), as expected when only slow drifts survive the cut. Solid for the cut, tentative for the rates (random epochs, not Gaia's scanning law). `triples/sb1_gamma_drift.py`, `sb1-gamma-drift.csv`, `sb1_triple_survival.py`, `sb1_fit_quality.py`, `sb1-fit-quality.csv` and their outputs.

## Masses from the eclipsing double-lined orbits

- **35 accepted systems get dynamical masses.** 37 accepted systems carry a Gaia DR3 SB2 orbit at the eclipse period (A9, A45, A54, A113 and R754 are at other periods). The eclipse bounds sin³ i at 0.97 (median; 0.80–0.995), so M sin³ i is within a few percent of the mass: median 1.10 M☉ per star (0.42–2.5), with 3% errors from Gaia's semi-amplitudes. The paper gives masses only for A179 and A204, and these reproduce them (1.07 + 1.15 and 1.05 + 1.08 M☉ at the paper's inclinations); it uses the same mass sum to reject A9's and A54's Gaia orbits, and applied to every SB2 the test also fails R729 and R1819. Solid. `masses/collect_sb2.py`, `collect_tic.py`, `sb2_masses.py`, `sb2-masses.csv`.
- **Gaia's SB2 semi-amplitudes are unbiased on these systems.** For 28 dwarfs with a TIC mass, the larger dynamical mass is 0.98 times the TIC mass (median; robust scatter 12%), a semi-amplitude scale of 0.99, rising toward 1.0 with the inclination correction. All 28 are at least as luminous as the mean main sequence predicts for their masses (median +0.16 dex; none more than 0.13 dex below), as stars of field ages should be. A114's 0.78 + 0.85 M☉ fits the paper's two 0.95 R☉ stars; its TIC temperature (6,863 K, no TIC mass) is the outlier. Solid. Same files.
- **R1819's Gaia SB2 orbit is wrong.** It gives e = 0.61 ± 0.006 at 3.63 d, where no binary under 3 d in the sample is eccentric, and 0.35 + 0.36 M☉ for a pair with BP−RP 0.78 and M_G 3.8: solar-type stars about three times heavier. The eclipse period agrees with Gaia's; the semi-amplitudes and eccentricity do not. Solid. `masses/sb2_masses.py`.
- **A45's 21.68-d Gaia SB2 is a real solar-mass pair, so its 31.15-d eclipses come from a second pair.** M sin³ i = 0.98 + 0.93 M☉ against a TIC mass of 1.12 M☉ (6,046 K, 4.5 L☉) puts i near 67–83°, short of the 81–88° that an eclipse at 21.68 d needs; the orbit is not spurious like A54's. A 2+2 quadruple if the eclipses are on target. Tentative. `masses/sb2_masses.py`.
- **A108 is probably a semi-detached Algol.** Gaia's q = 0.278 (1.50 + 0.42 M☉, circular, 6.42 d) cannot make its 21.4% eclipse with a main-sequence companion (at most 6–10%); the companion must be 0.67–1.8 R☉, 1.5–4 times its main-sequence radius, within a Roche lobe of 5.0 R☉. The 2.8% secondary (surface-brightness ratio 0.13) makes it much cooler than the F star, and the system is 0.5 dex more luminous than two main-sequence stars of these masses. Its check card already suspects an Algol. A lobe-filling companion would show an ellipsoidal signal of about 1–3% at P/2 (0.10 times its share of the light). Favoured. `masses/low_q_sb2.py`, `low-q-sb2-output.txt`.
- **A28's q = 0.50 needs an oversized companion too**: a 20% eclipse of an 8 R☉ giant needs a companion ≥ 3.6 R☉, six times the main-sequence radius of 0.58 M☉, or Gaia's semi-amplitudes are wrong (its orbit already fails on period and e cos ω). Open. Same files.

## Timing

- **The house's QLP eclipse times carry three clock faults, in sectors 14–15, 100–101 and 102 on.** The QLP FFI light curves (`hlsp_qlp_tess_ffi_*_tess_v01_llc.fits`) claim barycentric TDB times in every sector (`TIMEREF = 'SOLARSYSTEM'`). Each sector's mean timing residual, against ephemerides fitted to every other sector, is compared with the Rømer delay (Sun–Earth vector on the star's direction, up to ±8.3 min):
  - **Sectors 102–104 have no barycentric correction**: slopes k = 1.02 ± 0.13, 1.05 ± 0.12 and 1.08 ± 0.06 on the delay, offset ≈ 0; in 24 targets seen in both s90–99 and s102+ the timing step follows the delay with slope 1.05 ± 0.02, sign right in 24 of 24.
  - **Sectors 100 and 101 carry one frozen correction**, computed for TBJD 4069.5 and written into every cadence (errata's finding from the cadence spacing and from SPOC two-minute twins). The time to subtract is roemer(t) − roemer(4069.5): with no free parameter this leaves s100 at −0.11 min (χ² 11.2 for 8 targets, against 30.4 as barycentric) and s101 at −0.08 min (χ² 7.1 for 7, against 199). For these stars and dates it averages −1.2 min in s100 and −3.5 min in s101. SPOC two-minute and TESS-SPOC times in these sectors are correct; the absolute epoch keeps about 15 s of slack.
  - **Sectors 14 and 15 (QLP's TICA early-sector batch) carry a barycentric correction for the wrong sky position**, different per star. Re-timing the same events on TESS-SPOC light curves of the same FFIs (Fable 5: 45 of 73 events, 23 star-sectors; s16 controls agree to a constant 1.9 s) gives errors from −7.3 to +16.7 min, steady within a star-sector (median 28 s rms). As a multiple of the Rømer delay they span 0.4–4.1 (median 1.37), so a single factor (1.5, the average) leaves 96 s rms on the well-measured stars (χ² 214 for 18). The re-times replace the QLP times; the 26 events with no TESS-SPOC twin keep the 1.5× correction with 1.6 min added to their errors.
  After the correction no sector with six or more well-timed targets is off by more than 0.7 min (34 sectors). Correcting the times (`timing/time_offsets.py`) moves the period of 24 of the 82 `measurements.json` ephemerides with s101+ events by more than 3σ (more than 1σ in 57), led by R441 (10.6σ), R277, R230, R289, R255, R339, R314 and R469. Solid for 100–104, solid for 14–15 where TESS-SPOC exists. `timing/qlp_barycentre.py`, `time_offsets.py`, `clock_model_check.py`, `s1415-tspoc-times.csv`, `qlp-barycentre-output.txt`, `clock-model-check-output.txt`, `qlp-barycentre-periods.csv`; template timing in `timing/etv_survey.py`, `etv-events.csv`, `etv-summary.csv`.
- **82 of the 590 published ephemerides are built on the faulty s101+ times**, with the same period as their `measurements.json` (one has its epoch in s101+). Corrected, the period moves by more than one published σ in 51 and more than 3σ in 17; the predicted primary at BTJD 5000 (2028 August, TESS's next pass over much of this sky) moves by a median 6.5 min, more than 10 min in 29 and more than 30 min in 4, beyond twice the fit's own prediction error in 33 (R277 by 10σ, R255, R230, R339, R314, R469). `CHECKCARD.csv` holds none of them. The corrected period and epoch of every row, ready to replace the published ones, are in `published-ephemeris-shifts.csv`. Of the 40 that use s14–15 events and no s101+ ones, only R365 and R509 move by more than one published σ, and both are ephemerides whose own 2028 prediction error is days. The quoted errors are otherwise conservative: predicting each of 295 `measurements.json` ephemerides' last sector from its other sectors misses by a robust 0.29 quoted σ when that sector is before s101, but by 1.29 σ (10 of 56 beyond 3σ) when it is s101+ as given, and 0.45 σ (1 of 56) once corrected. Solid. `timing/published_ephemerides.py`, `published-ephemeris-shifts.csv`, `published-ephemerides-output.txt`.
- **A204's open timing variation is the clock, and so is A167's one-month swing.** The paper leaves A204's "about 6 min common to both eclipses across sectors 99–102, QLP-only" open between a third body and a pipeline systematic. The clock model predicts, after the same linear fit, +2.6 min in s99 and −2.4 in s102 (a −5.0 min swing); the report has +2.5 to +3 and −3 to −3.7, so 0.6 min rms is left of 1.9. In A167 the s100→s101 step of −2.6 and −2.9 min (primaries, secondaries), which the paper reads as a possibly short outer period, is the frozen correction (−2.3 predicted); a slower ±2 min variation between s38 and s64–65 remains (1.3–1.5 min rms) and still suggests a third body. The clock biases both systems' 2028 predictions by 3–4 min. A151's and A113's timing variations are in sectors the clock does not touch and are confirmed by TESS-SPOC. Solid for A204, solid for A167's step. `timing/a_system_etvs.py` (O−C values from the two supplement reports), `a-system-etvs-output.txt`.
- **Most apparent eclipse timing variations are the clock.** Of 287 targets timed in three or more sectors, 36 have sector means that disagree (reduced χ² > 20 with a 0.5 min floor); 15 remain after the correction. Four of those move by tens of minutes in an interior sector or jump between neighbouring sectors (measurement problems: R526, R365, R617, R675). The other eleven are timing-variation candidates, all with Gaia SB1 orbits at the eclipse period: R623, R371, R630, R423, R364, R269, R253, R186, R660, R596 and R478. The strongest, R623, is a light-travel orbit (Triples). Open. `timing/etv_candidates.py`, `etv-candidates.csv`.
- **R423, R630 and R371 have real, curved timing variations; R660's is spot noise.** Fable 5's times from every clean QLP sector (`timing/etv4-times.csv`, each file passed the cadence-curvature clock test; R630's two s15 times on TESS-SPOC) add 3–5 sectors per star. Linear ephemerides fail by χ² 370–1,600, and a steady period change fails for two of them:
  - **R423** (6.31 d, K dwarf): ±150 s, high in 2021 and 2025, low in 2023; quadratic χ² 650 for 13 dof, a circular light-travel orbit 9.2 for 11 with no jitter (P3 1,440–7,300 d at Δχ² < 4, best 4.7 yr; f(m3) = 0.0016 M☉, m3 ≥ 0.16 M☉).
  - **R630** (9.20 d, G dwarf): +330 s in 2019, −430 s in mid-2021, +390 s in 2024. The only one whose outer period is bounded: P3 = 1,440–2,580 d (best 1,693 d, 4.6 yr), semi-amplitude 435 s, a₁₂ sin i = 0.87 au, f(m3) = 0.031 M☉, so m3 ≥ 0.54 M☉ for a 1.06 M☉ primary. A quadratic (dP/dt = +7 s per year) is only mildly worse (χ² 25.5 for 17 against 14.8 for 15, both with 45 s jitter), but the two part by about an hour by mid-2026, so R630's next TESS sector decides. Over Gaia DR4's span the light-travel orbit predicts a systemic velocity that swings 10–13 km/s, lowest at the end of 2019 at every allowed outer period and highest in 2017.5–2018.5 for the best one (`triples/dr4_gamma_predictions.py`); R423's best orbit gives only ±2 km/s, its longer ones up to ±10.
  - **R371** (4.77 d, A star): +15 min in 2019, falling 9 min by 2021 and rising again, a quadratic leaves χ² 79 for 23; the timing allows outer periods of 1.5–1.6 yr or ≥ 5.2 yr, and Gaia's SB1 could not have been published on the short island (Triples), so P3 ≥ 5.2 yr, where a circular orbit needs m3 ≳ 0.7 M☉.
  - **R660** (18.02 d, a giant): eclipse times scatter by 224 s within sectors; with that jitter a linear ephemeris is acceptable (χ² 18.6 for 9) and the best light-travel orbit needs an impossible 25 M☉ tertiary.
  Every fit has as many outer-orbit parameters as the sector groups can pin (4–5 groups more than 60 d apart), so the periods are ranges; for R630 the inner 9.2 d and outer 4.6 yr periods allow a dynamical timing term of up to about 150 s if the outer orbit is eccentric. Tentative for R423 and R630, open for R371. `timing/etv_orbits.py` (SciPy), `etv-orbits-output.txt`.
- **Gaia's astrometry flags the same three triples.** Gaia DR3's non-single-star mask carries the astrometric bit for only 10 of the 557 accepted R systems with a Gaia row in a review `gaia.json`, and for 4 of the 275 timed in three or more sectors. Three of those four are R623, R630 and R423, the three with light-travel orbits (3 of the 14 timing-variation candidates with a Gaia row; one-sided Fisher p = 4 × 10⁻⁴); the other candidates carry only the spectroscopic bit and RUWE 0.8–1.5. An inner orbit of days moves the photocentre by microarcseconds, so the flag marks a wider companion, found independently of the timing. The fourth, R625 (2.60 d, RUWE 3.2 at a parallax of 1.1 mas), shows a mild timing excess over three sectors (reduced χ² 4.4, a 1.5 min range). R113, R114 and R173 (untimed) and R225, R238 and R393 (two sectors each) are the next timing targets. Gaia's acceleration or orbit solutions for these ten are not in this repository. RUWE itself does not separate the tiers (above 1.4 for 15% of confirmed and 14% of probable systems) or the two conjunction kinds, so it is no blend test here. Solid. `timing/astrometric_flag.py`, `gaia-ruwe-r-systems.csv`, `astrometric-flag-output.txt`.
  - **The RUWE excess has the size the light-travel orbits imply.** Projected onto the sky at Gaia's parallax, the timing orbits move the inner binary by 1.7–6 mas (a12 sin i alone) for R423, R630 and R623. A five-parameter fit over the DR3 span absorbs most of that; the rest, 0.1–0.5 mas for near-edge-on outer orbits (a main-sequence tertiary's light included), predicts RUWE 1.6–5 for a per-CCD along-scan error of 0.1 mas, against the observed 1.70, 3.69 and 3.00. The prediction moves by a factor of two or more with the outer period within its allowed range, the orbit's phase and node, the tertiary's light (which can cancel the photocentre motion) and the along-scan error scale, so RUWE does not choose between R623's branches or fix the outer inclinations. R371 (RUWE 0.88, no astrometric bit) stays open: some of its allowed outer periods leave no excess. Tentative. `timing/ruwe_wobble.py`, `ruwe-wobble.csv`, `ruwe-wobble-output.txt`.
  - **The other flagged systems' RUWE sets a timing floor, and R625's flat timing sits low against it.** Turned around, each RUWE fixes how far the photocentre must move for a given outer period. For a dark tertiary on a circular orbit coplanar with the eclipses, the eclipse delay must then reach a semi-amplitude of at least 1.1–1.9 min for R113, R114, R173, R238, R225 and R393 (per-CCD error 0.1 mas; half to twice that over 0.06–0.2 mas), at outer periods of 150–300 d, rising to 1.3–2.3 min at 1,000 d and 6–13 min at 3,000 d. R625's floor is 4.9 min (3.0–9.8), because it is the most distant (parallax 1.1 mas), yet its sector means in 2019 and twice in 2024 span only 1.4 min. Over random orientations (outer period 150–12,000 d, log-uniform), a tertiary that makes R625's RUWE leaves timing that flat in 20% of draws, mostly at low inclination (median 38°). So R625's wobble more likely belongs to an inclined or long-period tertiary, or to a neighbour, than to a coplanar companion of the eclipsing pair. A luminous tertiary would raise every floor. Tentative. `timing/astrometric_timing_floor.py`, `astrometric-timing-floor.csv`, `astrometric-timing-floor-output.txt`.
